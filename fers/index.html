Federal FERS

FERS explained: your pension, TSP and when you can retire

How the Federal Employees Retirement System works, from your retirement date to the annuity formula, the supplement, the TSP and how to apply.

Rules checked October 18, 2026, against opm.gov, tsp.gov, ssa.gov and the U.S. Code

Short answer

FERS covers most federal civilian employees hired in 1984 or later. It has three parts: a monthly pension called the basic annuity, the Thrift Savings Plan (TSP), and Social Security. Most people get a full annuity at their Minimum Retirement Age with 30 years, at 60 with 20 years, or at 62 with 5 years.

When can I retire with a full annuity?

You can retire with no reduction at any of these points (OPM: eligibility):

  • Your Minimum Retirement Age (MRA) with 30 years of service
  • Age 60 with 20 years
  • Age 62 with 5 years

You can also retire at your MRA with 10 to 29 years. That's called MRA+10, and your annuity is cut by 5% for each year you're under 62 (exactly 5/12 of 1% for each full month). If you have 20 or more years and your annuity starts at 60 or later, there's no cut. You can shrink or avoid the cut by putting off the start of your annuity, to any date before 62 (OPM: computation). MRA+10 retirees don't get the FERS supplement. More on retirement ages and MRA+10.

Law enforcement officers, firefighters, air traffic controllers and a few similar jobs have special provisions: retirement at 50 with 20 years of covered service, or at any age with 25 years (5 U.S.C. 8412). Most law enforcement officers and firefighters must leave at 57, or at 20 years if they're older by then (5 U.S.C. 8425). See FERS special provisions.

How is the annuity figured?

Your annuity is your high-3 (your highest average basic pay over three years in a row) times your years of service times a multiplier:

  • 1% for each year of service in most cases.
  • 1.1% if you retire at 62 or older with at least 20 years. That's a 10% bigger annuity.
  • Law enforcement officers, firefighters and air traffic controllers retiring under special provisions get 1.7% for each of the first 20 years and 1% for each year after.
Example

Kim is a made-up federal employee with a $95,000 high-3 who is married and wants a full survivor annuity for a spouse.

  1. Retire at 60 with 25 years: $95,000 × 1% × 25 = $23,750 a year, or about $1,979 a month.
  2. Full survivor annuity: the annuity is cut 10%, to $21,375 a year (about $1,781 a month). If Kim dies first, the spouse gets 50% of the unreduced $23,750, or $11,875 a year.
  3. FERS supplement until 62: if Kim's Social Security estimate at 62 is $1,800 a month, the supplement is about $1,800 × 25 ÷ 40 = $1,125 a month, paid until Kim turns 62.
  4. Retire at 62 with 27 years instead: $95,000 × 1.1% × 27 = $28,215 a year, about 19% more for life, but with no supplement and two fewer years of payments.

Taxes, health premiums and pay raises are left out. Your agency's estimate will be more exact.

Unused sick leave adds to your service for this calculation, but it doesn't help you reach the years you need to retire. OPM drops any partial month from your total service (5 U.S.C. 8415). See how sick leave adds to your annuity. Military service can also count if you pay a deposit for it before you retire. See the FERS military buyback.

While you work, you pay part of the cost from each paycheck: 0.8% of pay if you were hired before 2013, 3.1% if you were hired in 2013, and 4.4% if you were hired in 2014 or later (5 U.S.C. 8422). The annuity formula is the same for all three.

What is the FERS supplement?

If you retire before 62 at MRA with 30 years, at 60 with 20 years, or under special provisions, FERS pays an extra monthly amount until you turn 62. It's meant to stand in for Social Security until you can claim it. It equals your estimated Social Security at 62 times your years of FERS service, divided by 40. People who retire under MRA+10, deferred retirees and disability retirees don't get it (5 U.S.C. 8421, OPM: types of retirement).

The supplement has an earnings test. If you work after retiring and your wages or self-employment income go over Social Security's yearly limit, OPM cuts the supplement by $1 for every $2 over. The limit is $24,480 for 2026 and $25,560 for 2027 (SSA: earnings limits). The cut is based on the year before and starts in July of the following year. Your annuity itself isn't reduced (5 U.S.C. 8421a, OPM: annuity supplement survey). For special provisions retirees, earnings before their MRA don't count. See the supplement earnings test.

How the TSP grows

Your agency puts in 1% of your pay automatically. It also matches the first 5% you put in: dollar for dollar on the first 3%, and 50 cents on the dollar for the next 2%. If you put in 5%, your agency adds 5%, so 10% of your pay goes into your account (TSP: contributions). For 2026 you can put in up to $24,500, plus $8,000 more if you're 50 or older, or $11,250 more if you turn 60 to 63 this year (TSP Bulletin 25-3).

Starting in 2026, if your Social Security wages for the year before were over $150,000, any catch-up contributions must go in as Roth (after-tax) money (TSP Bulletin 25-3).

When you retire, you can take the TSP in monthly installments, single withdrawals, a life annuity, or a mix (TSP: withdrawals in retirement). Your TSP has no age rule tied to your annuity, so you can leave it invested after you retire. See TSP withdrawal options.

Yearly cost-of-living raises

Most FERS retirees start getting yearly cost-of-living raises at 62. People who retire under the special provisions for law enforcement, firefighters and air traffic controllers get them right away. When prices rise 2% or less, the raise matches prices. Between 2% and 3%, it's 2%. Above 3%, it's 1 point less than prices (5 U.S.C. 8462). FERS retirees got 2.0% for 2026 (OPM: cost-of-living adjustments). For 2027, Social Security's raise is 3.6% (SSA: 2027 fact sheet), so under the FERS formula, eligible FERS retirees get 1 point less: 2.6%. If you retired during the past year, your first raise is prorated. See FERS cost-of-living raises.

Protecting your spouse

A married retiree chooses a survivor annuity. A full survivor annuity costs 10% of your annuity and pays your spouse 50% of your unreduced annuity for life. A partial one costs 5% and pays 25%. Choosing less than a full survivor annuity needs your spouse's consent (OPM: computation).

  • Health coverage depends on it. A surviving spouse can keep FEHB only if a survivor annuity is paid to them and they were covered under your Self Plus One or Self and Family plan when you died.
  • Remarriage. A spouse's survivor annuity generally ends if they remarry before 55, unless you were married at least 30 years. Remarrying at 55 or later doesn't affect it.
  • Marriage length. A survivor generally must have been married to the employee for at least 9 months in total, unless the death was accidental.

Source: OPM: survivor benefits. See the FERS survivor benefit.

Health insurance in retirement

You can keep your FEHB health plan in retirement if you were enrolled for the 5 years of service just before you retire, or for all your service if that's shorter. The government keeps paying its share of the premium (5 U.S.C. 8905). Your share is the same as an employee's and is taken out of your annuity (OPM: annuitants). If you postpone an MRA+10 annuity, coverage stops when you leave and can restart when the annuity begins; you can bridge the gap with Temporary Continuation of Coverage for up to 18 months at full cost plus 2% (OPM: TCC). How FEHB fits with Medicare and other choices, and FEHB and FEGLI in retirement.

If you leave before you're eligible

If you leave federal service before you qualify for an immediate annuity, you can still get a deferred annuity later, as long as you have at least 5 years of civilian service and don't take a refund of your retirement contributions. It can start at 62 with 5 years, at your MRA with 30 years, or at your MRA with 10 years (reduced 5% a year under 62). Deferred retirees can't re-enroll in FEHB or FEGLI and don't get the supplement (OPM: types of retirement). That's a big reason to check your eligibility date before you leave.

How to apply

  1. Ask your agency's benefits or HR office for an annuity estimate, and how much notice they need.
  2. Pay any military service deposit to your agency while you're still working. It can't be paid after you retire.
  3. Fill out the retirement application. Most agencies now start it in OPM's Online Retirement Application and send you an email invitation (OPM: Online Retirement Application). The paper version is form SF 3107. Your HR office sends it to OPM through payroll.
  4. Apply for your TSP and Social Security separately.

Source: OPM: SF 3107 instructions. OPM's retirement line is 1-888-767-6738 (TTY 711), weekdays from 7:40 a.m. to 5 p.m. Eastern (OPM: contact retirement services).

What to do next

  1. Find your dates. Look up your MRA and your first unreduced date in the tables above.
  2. Check your service. Ask HR to confirm your service computation date, any military deposit and your sick leave balance.
  3. Get an annuity estimate. Ask for two dates, such as your earliest and the first date with no reduction.
  4. Plan the TSP. Decide how much income you'll need from it and when.
  5. Talk over the survivor annuity. It affects both your income and your spouse's health coverage.
  6. Check the whole picture. Try the quick Can I retire? tool or the full planner.

Common questions

How is the FERS annuity calculated?

High-3 × years of service × 1%, or 1.1% if you retire at 62 or older with at least 20 years. Special provisions employees get 1.7% for the first 20 years.

What is the FERS COLA for 2027?

With Social Security's 3.6% raise, the FERS formula gives 2.6% for eligible FERS retirees. Most regular FERS retirees don't get raises until 62.

Do I get the FERS supplement if I retire at 57 with 30 years?

Yes, if 57 is your MRA. The supplement runs until 62 and can be cut if you earn more than Social Security's limit.

Can I keep FEHB in retirement?

Yes, if you retire on an immediate annuity and were enrolled for the 5 years before it starts, or since your first chance if that's shorter.

How much does a survivor annuity cost?

A full one costs 10% of your annuity and pays your spouse 50% of the unreduced amount. A partial one costs 5% and pays 25%.

How much do FERS employees pay toward the pension?

0.8% of pay if hired before 2013, 3.1% if hired in 2013, and 4.4% if hired in 2014 or later. The annuity formula is the same.

Does my FERS pension reduce my Social Security?

No. FERS jobs pay Social Security tax, and the old reductions for public pensions have been repealed. Read more.
