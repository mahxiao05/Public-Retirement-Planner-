How long will my savings last?

Updated October 24, 2026

Short answer

It depends on three numbers: how much you have, how much you take out each month, and how fast the rest grows. Enter yours below. A pension and Social Security keep paying for life, so savings usually only need to fill the gap between them and what you spend.

Rules that affect how you draw down savings

  • Required minimum distributions. Tax-deferred accounts such as the TSP, 457(b), 403(b) and traditional IRAs must start paying out at age 73, or 75 if you were born in 1960 or later (IRS: RMD questions). If our plan has you taking out less than the required amount, the IRS rules win.
  • Taxes. Money from tax-deferred accounts is taxed as income when you take it out. Roth money that meets the rules comes out tax free. Plan your withdrawals before tax so you have enough after it.
  • The 10% early penalty. It usually doesn't apply to a 457(b), or to the TSP and most 401(k) and 403(b) plans if you leave your job in or after the year you turn 55 (50 for public safety workers). IRAs follow different rules. See our guide to TSP withdrawals.
  • Bad years early hurt more. A market drop in your first years of withdrawals does more damage than the same drop later. Many retirees keep a year or two of spending in cash or bonds for that reason.

To see savings next to your pension and Social Security, try Can I retire? or the full retirement planner.

See a worked example

Lee retires at 62 with $300,000 in savings and assumes 4% yearly growth.

  • Taking $1,500 a month, raised 2.5% each year for prices, the money lasts about 19 years and 5 months, until about age 81.
  • Taking $2,000 a month with the same raises, it lasts about 14 years, until about age 76.

An extra $500 a month shortens the money's life by more than five years. That's why it helps to start a pension and Social Security first and use savings only for the gap.

Common questions

What growth rate should I use?

No one knows future returns. Many planners use 3% to 5% a year for a mix of stocks and bonds. Try a lower rate too, so you can see what a weak market would do.

Should I raise the amount each year?

If you want your withdrawals to keep up with prices, yes. Leaving the raise at 0 keeps the dollar amount the same, which buys a little less each year.

What is the 4% rule?

It's a rule of thumb: take about 4% of your savings in the first year, then raise the amount with prices. It was based on past market returns and is a starting point, not a promise.

Do I have to take money out at a certain age?

Yes, for tax-deferred accounts. Required minimum distributions start at 73, or 75 if you were born in 1960 or later. Roth IRAs have no required withdrawals for the owner.

An illustration, not a promise. Real investment returns change from year to year, and we don't include taxes or fees. This isn't investment advice.
