When can I retire from Oregon PERS?

Rules checked October 18, 2026, against oregon.gov/pers

Short answer

Most PERS members can retire as early as 55 (police and fire at 50). For a pension with no early-retirement cut, Tier One general service members need to be 58, Tier Two 60, and OPSRP 65, unless they have enough years of service to go sooner. Each year early keeps a smaller share of the pension for life: about 91.8% at one year early and 65.7% at five years early.

Retirement ages by group

GroupFull benefitEarliest, with a cut
Tier One, general service58, or any age with 30 years55
Tier Two, general service60, or any age with 30 years55
OPSRP, general service65, or 58 with 30 years55
Tier One or Two, police and fire55, or 50 with 25 years50
OPSRP, police and fire55, or 53 with 25 years50

Source: Oregon PERS: Benefit component comparisons. Which group you're in depends on when you started: Tier One before 1996, Tier Two from 1996 to August 28, 2003, and OPSRP from August 29, 2003 on. Read more about the three groups.

For OPSRP police and fire members to use the police and fire ages, PERS says the service in the last 60 months before they become eligible must be police and fire service.

First, are you vested?

You can only retire with a pension once you're vested. Tier One and Tier Two members are vested after being an active member in each of five calendar years. OPSRP members are vested after five calendar years with at least 600 hours of qualifying work, or by working in a qualifying job on or after reaching normal retirement age (PERS: Benefit component comparisons). Your IAP is vested from the start.

Years count as retirement credit. For OPSRP, PERS gives one month of credit for each month in which you work most of the month, in a job that needs at least 600 hours a year (PERS: OPSRP annual statement FAQ). Your annual statement in Online Member Services shows your total.

How much does retiring early cost?

If you retire before your full-benefit age, PERS lowers your pension for life using its early retirement factors. The share you keep depends on how many years early you go, and the factors are the same for every plan:

Years before full-benefit ageShare of pension you keep
191.8%

377.5%

565.7%

Source: PERS Actuarial Equivalency Factors, January 1, 2026, Table 1. The table also lists factors by month, so retiring partway through a year falls between these numbers. Tier One members can be at most 3 years early (55 versus 58), and Tier Two at most 5. OPSRP general service members under 30 years can be as much as 10 years early (55 versus 65), which is why the early cut matters most for OPSRP. An OPSRP member with 30 years who retires at 55 is only 3 years early, because their full-benefit age is 58.

Example: three dates compared

Example

Sam is a made-up Tier Two general service member who will turn 57 next year with 25 years of service, so Sam won't reach 30 years before 60. With the service and pay Sam will have at each age, PERS's formula gives these pensions before any early cut: $3,000 a month at 57, $3,130 at 58, and $3,390 at 60.

  1. Retire at 57: 3 years early, so Sam keeps 77.5%. $3,000 × 0.775 = $2,325 a month.
  2. Retire at 58: 2 years early, so 84.3%. $3,130 × 0.843 = about $2,639 a month.
  3. Retire at 60: no cut. $3,390 a month.

Waiting three years raises Sam's monthly pension by about $1,065, or 46%, for life. The trade-off is three fewer years of payments, which Sam would need to cover from pay or savings. The made-up pension amounts stand in for Sam's own PERS estimate.

Working longer helps twice

Each extra year adds a year of service, which raises the pension, and before your full-benefit age it also shrinks the early-retirement cut. For many people the last few years before full-benefit age add the most. Tier One and Tier Two members whose employers take part in the sick leave program can also add half of their unused sick leave hours to their final average salary, which makes saving sick leave worth something at retirement. See unused sick leave and your PERS pension.

Your IAP has no age rule

Your Individual Account Program (IAP) is a separate savings account. Since January 1, 2011, you must retire from your pension and your IAP at the same time. You can take the IAP as a lump sum or in installments over 5, 10, 15 or 20 years, or over your expected lifetime (PERS: What is the IAP?). Withdrawals before 59½ may face a 10% IRS penalty unless an exception applies. See IAP payout options.

Picking the exact date

  • The first of a month. A PERS retirement date is always the first day of a month.
  • Apply within 90 days. Turn in your retirement application within the 90 days before your retirement date, and tell your employer you're retiring (PERS: steps to retire).
  • First check within 92 days. PERS says your first payment should arrive within 92 days of your retirement date, back-dated to that date. Plan your cash for the gap.
  • Watch your birthday month. Retiring a few months before a birthday that ends the early cut costs you for life. Check whether waiting until the first of the month after your birthday helps.
  • Going back to work. If you retire early and want to work unlimited hours for a PERS employer, PERS says you need a complete break of at least six full months first. Most limits on retirees working for PERS employers are lifted through December 31, 2034 (PERS: Tier One/Tier Two preretirement guide). See working after retiring from PERS.

What to do next

  1. Confirm your plan and credit. Sign in to Online Member Services and check your plan, your years of service and your vesting.
  2. Find your full-benefit date. Use the table above, counting years of service as well as age.
  3. Request two estimates. Ask PERS for estimates at your earliest date and your full-benefit date.
  4. Plan health coverage. If you'll retire before 65, see PERS retiree health insurance and health insurance before Medicare.
  5. Compare dates. Run the PERS estimator or the quick Can I retire? tool.

Common questions

Can I retire from PERS at 55?

Yes, if you're vested, but unless you have 30 years (Tier One or Tier Two) the pension is cut for life. At 55, Tier One keeps 77.5%, Tier Two 65.7%, and OPSRP general service under 30 years 44.0%.

Can a Tier One or Tier Two member retire before 55 with 30 years?

Yes. PERS lists 30 years of service as a full-benefit point for Tier One and Tier Two general service at any age.

What is the full retirement age for OPSRP?

65 for general service, or 58 with 30 years. For police and fire it's 55, or 53 with 25 years.

Is the early retirement cut permanent?

Yes. The reduced amount is your pension for life. Yearly cost-of-living raises are added to the reduced amount.

Can I take my IAP early and keep working?

Generally no. Since 2011 you retire from your pension and your IAP at the same time.

When will I get my first PERS check?

PERS says within 92 days of your retirement date, with payments back-dated to that date.

Estimates, not advice. Ask PERS for an official estimate before you choose a date: call 888-320-7377 or use Online Member Services.
