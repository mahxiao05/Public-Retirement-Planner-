When should I start Social Security?

Rules checked October 18, 2026, against ssa.gov, irs.gov and medicare.gov

Short answer

You can start at any age from 62 to 70. If you were born in 1960 or later, starting at 62 pays 30% less than at 67, and waiting until 70 pays 24% more. The choice lasts for life, and it also sets what a surviving spouse would get. In our example below, waiting from 62 to 67 comes out ahead in total dollars at about age 79.

Your full retirement age

Your full retirement age is when you can get 100% of your primary insurance amount, the monthly figure Social Security works out from your 35 highest years of earnings. It depends on the year you were born:

Born inFull retirement ageAt 62 you get
1943 to 19546675%
195566 and 2 months74.2%
195666 and 4 months73.3%
195766 and 6 months72.5%
195866 and 8 months71.7%
195966 and 10 months70.8%
1960 or later6770%

Source: SSA: Retirement age and benefit reduction. If you were born on January 1, Social Security uses the row for the year before. If you were born on the 1st of any other month, it figures your age as if your birthday were in the month before.

How the amount changes

  • Starting early: your benefit drops 5/9 of 1% for each month before full retirement age, for up to 36 months, and 5/12 of 1% for each month beyond that (SSA: early retirement).
  • Waiting: it rises 2/3 of 1% for each month after full retirement age, which is 8% a year, until 70. These are called delayed retirement credits (SSA: delayed retirement credits). There's no gain from waiting past 70.
  • Yearly raises apply either way. Your benefit gets the same cost-of-living raise as everyone else's, whenever you start. The raise for 2027 is 3.6% (SSA: 2027 fact sheet).

Example and break-even ages

Example

Chris is a made-up public employee born in 1962, so Chris's full retirement age is 67. Chris's full-retirement-age amount is $2,000 a month. Leaving out yearly raises and taxes:

  1. Start at 62: 70% of $2,000 = $1,400 a month.
  2. Start at 67: $2,000 a month.
  3. Start at 70: 124% of $2,000 = $2,480 a month.
  4. 62 versus 67: by 67, starting at 62 has paid 60 months × $1,400 = $84,000. Waiting pays $600 a month more after that, so it takes $84,000 ÷ $600 = 140 months (about 11 years and 8 months) to catch up. That's about age 78 and 8 months.
  5. 67 versus 70: by 70, starting at 67 has paid 36 × $2,000 = $72,000. Waiting pays $480 a month more, so it catches up after 150 months, about age 82 and 6 months.

If Chris lives past those ages, waiting pays more in total. If not, starting earlier does. Because raises are a percentage, they make the bigger check grow faster, which moves the real break-even a little earlier.

Things to weigh

  • Health and family history. Waiting pays off if you live into your 80s. Starting early can make sense if your health is poor.
  • Your spouse. If you earned more, your benefit becomes your spouse's survivor benefit after you die. Waiting raises it for both of you. A widow or widower at full retirement age can get 100% of what the worker was getting (SSA: if you are the survivor). A spouse can get up to half of your full-retirement-age amount while you're alive, but that half doesn't grow if you wait past full retirement age (SSA: benefits for a spouse). See spousal benefits and survivor benefits.
  • Working. If you start before full retirement age and keep working, Social Security holds back $1 for every $2 you earn over $25,560 in 2027. In the year you reach full retirement age, it holds back $1 for every $3 over $67,920, counting only months before your birthday month. From your full retirement age on, there's no limit (SSA: earnings limits). Money held back isn't lost: your benefit is raised later to make up for it. See the earnings test explained.
  • Your pension. A pension can cover your bills while you wait, so many public employees retire first and start Social Security later. FERS retirees who get the supplement until 62 should know it's also cut by earnings over the limit. See the FERS supplement earnings test.
  • Taxes. Up to 85% of your benefit can be taxed when your other income is high, and a pension counts toward that income. Social Security says you may owe tax if your combined income is over $25,000 filing alone or $32,000 filing jointly (SSA: income taxes and your benefit). For 2025 through 2028, people 65 or older can also take an extra $6,000 deduction, which phases out above $75,000 of income ($150,000 for joint filers) (IRS: enhanced deduction for seniors). See are Social Security benefits taxed?
  • Your savings. Spending from an IAP, TSP or 457 plan for a few years while you wait for a larger check is a common trade. It lowers your savings early but raises a payment that lasts for life and grows with prices.

Timing rules that trip people up

  • Apply ahead. You can apply up to 4 months before you want payments to start (SSA: how to apply).
  • Back pay is limited. If you're past full retirement age, you can ask for benefits to start up to 6 months before you apply, but never before your full retirement age. Asking for back pay also gives up the credits you'd have earned in those months (SSA: delayed retirement credits).
  • Medicare doesn't wait. Medicare starts at 65 even if you put off Social Security. If you aren't getting Social Security yet, you must sign up yourself during the 7 months around your 65th birthday (Medicare: when coverage starts). See when to sign up for Medicare.
  • Payments are a month behind. Social Security pays each month's benefit in the following month.

What to do next

  1. Get your numbers. Your statement at ssa.gov/myaccount shows your estimate at 62, at full retirement age and at 70.
  2. Do the same for your spouse. The higher earner's start age matters most for the survivor.
  3. Work out the gap. If you retire before you start Social Security, add up what your pension and savings must cover each month until it starts.
  4. Mark your Medicare window. Put the 7 months around your 65th birthday on your calendar.
  5. Compare start ages. Try the quick Can I retire? tool, which shows Social Security at 62, 67 and 70.

Our planner shows your Social Security at every start age next to your pension, so you can see the trade-off.

Common questions

Can I change my mind after I start Social Security?

The start age generally sets your benefit for life, so treat it as a lasting choice. Social Security can tell you about any options for withdrawing an application in your own situation.

Does my government pension lower my Social Security?

No. The Social Security Fairness Act ended the rules that used to cut benefits for people with public pensions. Read what changed.

Is it better to take Social Security at 62 and invest it?

That only comes out ahead if your investments beat the guaranteed, inflation-adjusted increase you'd get from waiting, which is 8% a year after full retirement age. Few safe investments do that.

What happens if I work part time after starting at 62?

Social Security holds back $1 for every $2 you earn over $25,560 in 2027. Your pension doesn't count as earnings for this test.

Do I have to start Social Security to get Medicare?

No. Medicare starts at 65 either way, but if you aren't getting Social Security yet you have to sign up for Medicare yourself.

When does my spouse get a survivor benefit?

A widow or widower can start a survivor benefit as early as 60, at a reduced rate, or get 100% of your benefit at their own full retirement age.
