  3. Single-life or survivor pension: how to choose

Should I take a single-life pension or a survivor option?

Rules checked October 18, 2026, against oregon.gov/pers, drs.wa.gov, opm.gov, calpers.ca.gov and ssa.gov

Short answer

A single-life pension pays you the most each month, but payments stop when you die. A joint-and-survivor option pays you less so that a spouse or other beneficiary keeps getting a monthly check, often 50% or 100% of yours, for the rest of their life. In most public plans the choice is made once, at retirement, and can only be changed in a few narrow situations.

What the options are

Every public pension plan in this guide offers the same basic trade. You can take the full monthly amount for your own life only, or you can accept a smaller amount in exchange for a promise that someone else keeps getting paid after you die.

  • Single life (also called "Option 1," "unmodified" or "no survivor benefit"): the highest payment you can get. It ends at your death. Some plans refund any of your own contributions that haven't been paid out yet, but no monthly check continues.
  • Joint and survivor: your payment is reduced for life. When you die, your survivor (also called a joint annuitant or beneficiary) gets a set share of your payment, such as 50%, 66.67% or 100%, for the rest of their life.
  • Period certain: some plans offer a guarantee that payments continue for a set number of years (for example 15 years) even if you die early. After that period, nothing more is paid.

The survivor's payment is a percentage of your reduced payment in most plans, not of the full single-life amount. Federal FERS is an exception: the survivor percentage is figured on your unreduced annuity.

Options by retirement system

The names differ from plan to plan. Your plan's own handbook has the full list.

Oregon PERS and FERS each have rules of their own. For the details, see our guides to Oregon PERS survivor options and the FERS survivor benefit.

How the reduction works

A survivor option costs something because the plan expects to pay for two lifetimes instead of one. How it figures that cost depends on the system.

Plans that use an age-based factor

Oregon PERS, Washington DRS and CalPERS reduce your payment using actuarial factors (tables based on life expectancy). The reduction depends on your age, your survivor's age and the percentage you choose. A younger survivor or a higher percentage means a bigger cut. DRS shows this example on its PERS Plan 2 page, for a member whose single-life benefit is $2,122 a month:

DRS optionYou getSurvivor gets
Single life$2,122None
Joint and 100%$1,763$1,763
Joint and 66.67%$1,869$1,233
Joint and 50%$1,926$963

Your own numbers will differ. The only way to see your real reduction is an official estimate from your plan, run with your survivor's actual birth date.

Some plans limit choices when the survivor is not your spouse and is much younger. DRS, for example, allows any option when the age gap is 10 years or less, only Option 3 or 4 when it is 11 to 24 years, and only Option 3 when it is 25 years or more. Oregon PERS reduces Option 2 when a non-spouse beneficiary is more than 10 years younger, to meet IRS limits.

FERS uses a flat percentage

FERS does not use age tables for a spouse. According to OPM, a maximum survivor annuity cuts your annuity by 10% and pays your spouse 50% of your unreduced annuity. A partial survivor annuity cuts it by 5% and pays 25%. Because the cost is the same whatever your spouse's age, the FERS math tends to favor survivor coverage when the spouse is much younger.

Example

Lee is a made-up FERS retiree with an unreduced annuity of $3,000 a month, married to a spouse three years younger.

  1. No survivor annuity: Lee gets $3,000. The spouse gets nothing from FERS after Lee dies.
  2. Partial: Lee gets $3,000 minus 5% ($150), or $2,850. The spouse would get 25% of $3,000, or $750.
  3. Maximum: Lee gets $3,000 minus 10% ($300), or $2,700. The spouse would get 50% of $3,000, or $1,500.

The maximum option costs Lee $300 a month to protect a $1,500 monthly benefit. Cost-of-living raises would change both numbers over time.

Things to weigh

Ages and health of both of you

The survivor option pays off if your survivor outlives you by many years. It costs you money, in hindsight, if your survivor dies first (unless your plan has a pop-up, below) or if you both live a long time together and the survivor's years alone are short. Family history and current health matter more than averages.

Your survivor's other income

Ask what your spouse would live on if your pension stopped tomorrow. If they have their own pension, savings and Social Security, a single-life choice may leave them fine. If your pension is the main household income, losing it can be a hard shock, because many costs (housing, insurance, utilities) do not drop by half when one person dies.

Social Security survivor benefits

Social Security pays its own survivor benefit, separate from your pension choice. A surviving spouse can get up to 100% of the deceased worker's benefit at the survivor's full retirement age, or a reduced amount starting at 71.5% at age 60 (SSA: What you could get from survivor benefits). The survivor gets the higher of their own benefit or the survivor benefit, not both. So when one spouse dies, the household loses one of its two Social Security checks. That loss is part of the gap the pension survivor option can fill. See our guide on when to start Social Security for how claiming age affects the survivor amount.

Health insurance tied to the survivor annuity (FERS)

For federal retirees this can be the deciding factor. OPM says that if you choose no survivor annuity, your spouse's health benefits end at your death. A surviving spouse can keep Federal Employees Health Benefits (FEHB) coverage only if a monthly survivor annuity is payable, you had a Self Plus One or Self and Family enrollment when you died, and the spouse was covered under it (OPM: Survivor benefits). This is one reason many federal retirees choose at least the partial survivor annuity. State plans have their own retiree health rules, so ask your plan how survivors keep coverage.

Spousal consent

Most plans protect spouses. Under FERS, a married retiree needs the spouse's consent to choose a partial or no survivor annuity. DRS requires the spouse's notarized consent to choose single life or to name someone other than the spouse. Check the consent rules on your plan's application.

The life insurance alternative ("pension maximization")

Some insurance agents suggest a strategy often called pension maximization: take the larger single-life pension and use part of the extra money to buy a life insurance policy on your life. If you die first, the policy pays your spouse a lump sum to replace the lost pension.

It can work, but the risks are real:

  • The policy must stay in force. If premiums rise, or you stop paying in a tight year, the coverage can lapse and your spouse is left with nothing.
  • The amount may be too small. A survivor pension usually lasts for life and may get yearly cost-of-living raises. A fixed lump sum has to be invested and drawn down carefully to last as long.
  • Health insurance can be lost. Under FERS, giving up the survivor annuity also gives up your spouse's right to continue FEHB. No life policy replaces that.
  • Health and age affect the price. Coverage may be expensive or unavailable for someone in their 60s with health problems.
  • Commissions. The person suggesting the strategy may earn a commission on the policy. Ask how they are paid.

If you consider this, get the full survivor-option estimate from your plan first, and compare it with written insurance quotes, not illustrations.

Pop-up provisions and changing your mind

A pop-up (or switch-back) means your payment rises to the single-life amount if your survivor dies before you. Not every option has one.

SystemIf your survivor dies first
Oregon PERSOptions 2A and 3A let you switch to Option 1 by written request (also after a divorce from a spouse beneficiary). Options 2 and 3 do not.
Washington DRSContact DRS and your benefit can be changed to the single-life amount.
FERSOPM "may be able to increase" your annuity once it has proof of death. After a divorce, the reduction is removed unless a court order requires a former-spouse benefit.
CalPERSOptions 2 and 3 rise to the Unmodified Allowance. Options 2W and 3W do not.

Sources: Oregon PERS pre-retirement guide, DRS: PERS Plan 2, OPM: Death of spouse, OPM: Life events, CalPERS: Changing your beneficiary or monthly benefit.

How permanent is the choice?

Treat the decision as permanent. The short windows that exist are:

  • Oregon PERS: you can change your option within 60 days after your first benefit payment. The change is retroactive and you repay any overpayment.
  • FERS: within 30 days after your first regular annuity payment you can reduce or cancel a spouse election. Within 18 months of your annuity start date you can add a spouse election or raise partial to maximum. If you marry after retiring, you have 2 years from the marriage to elect a survivor annuity for the new spouse.
  • Washington DRS: the choice is generally final. If you marry after retiring, you may have a one-time chance to add the new spouse, requested between your first and second year of marriage.
  • CalPERS: you can change your lifetime beneficiary only after a qualifying life event, such as marriage or the beneficiary's death.

What to do next

  1. Get an official estimate for every option. Ask your plan to run each survivor option with your survivor's real birth date. Oregon PERS and DRS members can request estimates through their online accounts. Federal employees get estimates from their agency's HR office.
  2. Write down your survivor's income both ways. List their Social Security (as a survivor), their own pension and savings, with and without your survivor benefit.
  3. Check health coverage. Ask your plan, in writing, whether your survivor keeps retiree health coverage under each option.
  4. Read the system guide. See Oregon PERS survivor options or the FERS survivor benefit for plan-specific rules.

Common questions

Does my spouse automatically get my pension if I die?

No. In most public plans your spouse gets a monthly pension only if you chose a survivor option at retirement. With single life, payments stop at your death, though some plans refund any unpaid contributions.

Can I name someone other than my spouse as survivor?

Often yes, but married members usually need the spouse's written consent, and plans may limit the choices when a non-spouse survivor is much younger than you.

What happens to the reduction if my survivor dies first?

It depends on the option. Pop-up options (Oregon PERS 2A and 3A, CalPERS 2 and 3, and DRS joint options) raise your payment back to the single-life amount. Options without a pop-up keep the reduced payment.

Is the FERS survivor benefit worth it?

There is no single answer. The FERS cost (10% for the maximum) does not change with your spouse's age, and it is required for your spouse to keep FEHB health coverage. Those two facts lead many federal retirees to keep at least the partial benefit.

Can I change my option after I retire?

Only in short windows or after specific life events. Oregon PERS allows a change within 60 days after the first payment; FERS has 30-day and 18-month windows; DRS and CalPERS allow changes mainly after marriage or a beneficiary's death.
