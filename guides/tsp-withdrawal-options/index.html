How do I take money out of my TSP in retirement?

Rules checked October 18, 2026, against tsp.gov

Short answer

After you leave federal service you can take installments (monthly, quarterly or yearly), partial withdrawals of at least $1,000, buy a life annuity, or leave the money invested, and you can mix these. There's no 10% early withdrawal penalty if you separate in or after the year you turn 55 (50 for public safety employees). Required minimum distributions start at 73, or 75 if you were born in 1960 or later, and Roth TSP money is not subject to them.

Your withdrawal options

The Thrift Savings Plan (TSP) lets separated participants use these options alone or in combination (TSP: Withdrawals in retirement and TSP: Distributions booklet):

OptionKey rules
Leave it inAllowed if your vested balance is $200 or more. Keeps growing tax-deferred.
InstallmentsMonthly, quarterly or yearly. At least $25 each, or based on life expectancy.
Partial withdrawalAt least $1,000. Can be taken while installments continue.
AnnuityAt least $3,500. Can't be changed or canceled.
Total withdrawalEmpties the account and ends installments.

Installments keep your money invested in the funds you choose. You can pick a fixed dollar amount, or let the TSP figure a payment from IRS life expectancy tables, recalculated each January. You can stop or change installments at any time, though some changes mean stopping the old series and starting a new one. Payments continue until you stop them or the balance hits zero.

Partial withdrawals are useful for one-time needs such as a new roof or a car, or for topping up cash when the FERS supplement ends at 62.

Leaving money in is a real option. You aren't required to take anything until required minimum distributions begin. You can also roll money into the TSP from eligible employer plans and traditional IRAs after you leave. Withdrawals can't be reversed once processed, so check requests carefully.

Buying an annuity

You can use all or part of your balance (at least $3,500, counted separately for traditional and Roth money) to buy a life annuity from the TSP's annuity vendor. It pays a monthly amount for life, either for you alone or jointly with another person. The TSP's annuities fact sheet explains the payment and survivor choices.

The trade-off: once purchased, "you cannot change or cancel the purchase," and the money is no longer yours to invest or leave to heirs (beyond any features you choose). The payment depends on interest rates when you buy. The TSP posts current and past rates on its historical annuity rates page and has an annuity calculator.

Spouse's rights

If you are married, under FERS, and your balance is over $3,500, your spouse is entitled by law to a joint life annuity with a 50% survivor benefit, level payments and no cash refund. To choose anything else (installments, a partial withdrawal, or a different annuity), your spouse must sign a consent. Consent is also required to change installment amounts or frequency. Exceptions are rare (TSP: Distributions booklet). Plan for this paperwork, especially if your spouse travels or lives elsewhere.

The 10% penalty and the age 55 rule

Taxable withdrawals before age 59½ normally carry a 10% IRS early withdrawal penalty on top of income tax. The TSP's tax booklet lists the exceptions that matter most to federal retirees (TSP: Tax rules about TSP payments):

  • Separation at 55: payments made after you separate "during or after the year you reach age 55."
  • Public safety employees: payments after you separate during or after the year you reach 50, or with 25 years of service. This covers federal public safety jobs such as those under the special retirement provisions. Check the booklet for the exact definition.
  • Life expectancy installments: substantially equal payments over your life expectancy. Be careful: if you stop them, switch to fixed amounts or take another withdrawal within five years or before 59½ (whichever is later), the penalty can apply after the fact.
  • Disability and death.

These exceptions apply to money paid from the TSP. If you move money to an IRA, the IRA's own rules apply instead, so check how the penalty exceptions work there before you roll money out in your 50s.

Traditional and Roth money

Many people have both. For any withdrawal, you can take from traditional only, from Roth only, or pro rata in the same proportions as your account. For installments, you choose which source to draw first, and when it runs out, payments continue from the other.

Roth TSP earnings are tax-free only in a qualified distribution, which needs both of these (TSP: Tax rules booklet):

  • 5 years have passed since January 1 of the year you made your first Roth TSP contribution, and
  • you are 59½ or older, permanently disabled, or deceased.

Your own Roth contributions always come out tax-free. In a non-qualified withdrawal, the earnings portion is taxable.

Required minimum distributions

Required minimum distributions (RMDs) are the smallest amount the IRS makes you take each year once you reach a set age and have left federal service. The TSP booklet gives these ages:

BornRMD age
Before 196073
1960 or later75

Your first RMD is due by April 1 of the year after you reach your RMD age (or after you separate, if later). Only your traditional balance counts. The booklet says "Roth money in your account isn't subject to RMDs." If your installments don't cover the RMD, the TSP sends a supplemental payment. RMD amounts can't be rolled over.

Taxes and withholding

Traditional TSP withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. The TSP withholds federal tax based on the type of payment (TSP: Tax rules booklet):

Payment typeFederal withholding
Partial withdrawal or installments for less than 10 years20% (mandatory unless rolled over)
Installments for 10+ years or life expectancyWithheld as if you're single with zero exemptions, unless you change it
RMDs and other nonperiodic payments10% default, can change 0% to 100%

Withholding is a prepayment, not your final tax. Your real bill depends on your total income, including your FERS annuity and Social Security. The TSP does not withhold state or local tax, but it reports payments to your state if your state has an income tax.

Example

Jamie is a made-up FERS employee born in 1969 who retires at 57 in 2026, just after reaching an MRA of 56 and 10 months, with 30 years, a $400,000 traditional TSP balance and a $60,000 Roth TSP balance (first Roth contribution in 2014).

  1. Penalty: Jamie separated in or after the year of turning 55, so TSP withdrawals avoid the 10% penalty even before 59½.
  2. Plan: Jamie sets up monthly installments of $1,500 from the traditional balance first. Because Jamie is married, Jamie's spouse signs the consent form.
  3. Withholding: fixed installments of $1,500 a month would take about 22 years to use up the traditional balance, so they count as periodic payments, withheld as if Jamie were single with zero exemptions unless Jamie changes it.
  4. At 62: the FERS supplement stops. Jamie takes a $15,000 partial withdrawal to bridge to a later Social Security start. 20% ($3,000) is withheld.
  5. Roth: withdrawals after Jamie turns 59½ are qualified, since the five-year clock started January 1, 2014.
  6. RMDs: born in 1969, Jamie's RMD age is 75. Only the traditional balance counts.

What to do next

  1. Log in and check your balances. Know your traditional and Roth amounts.
  2. Mind the year you separate. If you're close to 55 (or 50 in public safety), the calendar year you leave can matter for the penalty.
  3. Get spousal consent ready if you're married and want anything other than the joint annuity.
  4. Read the two booklets. The TSP's Distributions and Tax Rules booklets are the official word.
  5. Ask a tax professional about withholding and Roth timing for your situation.

Common questions

Can I leave my money in the TSP after I retire?

Yes, as long as your vested balance is at least $200. You don't have to withdraw anything until required minimum distributions begin.

What is the TSP age 55 rule?

If you leave federal service in or after the year you turn 55, TSP payments aren't hit with the 10% early withdrawal penalty. For public safety employees the age is 50, or any age with 25 years of service.

When do TSP required minimum distributions start?

At 73 if you were born before 1960, and at 75 if you were born in 1960 or later, once you've left federal service. Roth TSP money is not included.

How much tax does the TSP withhold?

20% on partial withdrawals and installments under 10 years, wage-style withholding on longer installments, and a 10% default on RMDs. The TSP doesn't withhold state tax.

Does my spouse have to sign for a TSP withdrawal?

If you're married under FERS and your balance is over $3,500, yes, unless you take the default joint annuity with a 50% survivor benefit.

Can I take money from only my Roth TSP?

Yes. You can choose traditional only, Roth only, or pro rata for withdrawals, and choose which source installments draw from first.

Can I change my TSP installments?

Yes, you can stop or change them at any time. Some changes require stopping the current installments and starting new ones.

General information, not advice. We aren't part of the TSP or the Federal Retirement Thrift Investment Board. The TSP's ThriftLine and booklets give official answers, and a tax professional can help with your tax situation.
