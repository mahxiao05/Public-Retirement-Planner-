Where to begin with retirement planning

A few years from retiring and not sure where to start? Follow these six steps in order. Each one tells you where to get your official numbers.

Rules checked October 18, 2026, against oregon.gov/pers, opm.gov, tsp.gov, ssa.gov and medicare.gov

Short answer

Find out which retirement system you're in and get your official records. Then estimate your income at two or three possible retirement dates, line up health insurance until Medicare at 65, and apply about three months before your last day. Start about three years out if you can, so there's time to fix records and buy back service.

  1. Find out which retirement system you're in

    Your pension comes from the system your job pays into. Most public employees also pay into Social Security. Check your pay stub for "Social Security" or "OASDI".

    • Oregon state, school, city or county job: Oregon PERS. Your plan (Tier One, Tier Two or OPSRP) depends on the year you started. Oregon PERS explained
    • Federal civilian job, hired in 1984 or later: FERS, which includes the TSP. FERS explained
    • Washington state or local job: the Washington Department of Retirement Systems. Washington DRS explained
    • Everyone who paid Social Security tax: Social Security explained

    Many people have more than one. A common mix is a pension, a savings account that comes with it (the IAP for PERS, the TSP for FERS), Social Security, and sometimes a voluntary 457 plan or an IRA. Write down every account and the agency that runs it.

  2. Get your official records

    Every estimate is only as good as the numbers you start with. Gather these before anything else:

    • Oregon PERS: sign in to Online Member Services on oregon.gov/pers to check your years of service and request a pension estimate.
    • FERS: ask your agency's benefits or HR office for an annuity estimate, and check your TSP balance at tsp.gov.
    • Social Security: open your statement at ssa.gov/myaccount. Look for missing years in your earnings record. They are easier to fix now than after you retire.

    Things to look for in those records:

    • Years of service. Your service credit drives the pension. Check for gaps, part-time years and any earlier job that might count.
    • Service you could add. Federal employees may be able to pay a deposit for military service, which must be done before retiring (FERS military buyback). Unused sick leave can also add to a FERS annuity (FERS sick leave credit) and to some PERS pensions (PERS unused sick leave).
    • Your plan or tier. For PERS, the year you started sets your plan and your full-benefit age. See Tier One, Tier Two and OPSRP.
    • Beneficiaries. Make sure the people named on each account are the ones you want.

  3. Pick two or three possible retirement dates

    Most people compare their earliest possible date with the first date they get a full pension. Some ages matter more than others:

    • Your plan's full-benefit age, when the early-retirement cut goes away. See PERS ages or FERS ages.
    • 62, when Social Security can start. Under FERS it's also when the 1.1% multiplier can begin.
    • 65, when Medicare starts. Retiring earlier means paying for health insurance on your own.

  4. Estimate your income at each date

    Add up your pension, your savings account (the IAP or TSP) and Social Security for each date, then compare the total with what you spend each month. Pensions are taxed as income, so plan for taxes too.

    Example

    Morgan is a made-up Oregon PERS member, 60, comparing retiring now with retiring at 62. Morgan's spending is $5,200 a month. Using official estimates (the numbers here are made up):

    1. Retire at 60: pension $3,100 a month. Social Security not started. That leaves a $2,100 monthly gap, plus health insurance until 65, to cover from the IAP and other savings.
    2. Retire at 62: pension $3,450 a month (two more years of service, and no early cut). Starting Social Security at 62 would add $1,500, for $4,950 a month, a $250 gap.
    3. The trade-off: retiring at 60 means two more years of free time, but about $50,000 more drawn from savings before 62, plus a smaller pension for life.

    Neither answer is right for everyone. The point is to see both numbers side by side before choosing.

    How you take your savings matters too. The IAP can be paid as a lump sum or in installments (IAP payout options), and the TSP has its own choices (TSP withdrawal options). Some people use savings to cover the years before Social Security so they can start it later at a higher amount. See when to start Social Security, and check how Social Security is taxed.

  5. Plan your health insurance until 65

    If you retire before 65, you need coverage until Medicare starts. Federal retirees can usually keep FEHB. PERS retirees can buy a PERS retiree plan. Compare your health insurance choices.

  6. Apply on time

    • Oregon PERS: turn in your retirement application within the 90 days before your retirement date, and tell your employer you're retiring. A PERS retirement date is always the first of a month, and PERS says the first payment arrives within 92 days of it (PERS: steps to retire).
    • FERS: your agency's HR office starts your application, usually in OPM's Online Retirement Application, and sends you an email invitation (OPM: Online Retirement Application). The paper version is form SF 3107. Ask HR how much notice they need. You apply for the TSP and Social Security separately (OPM: SF 3107 instructions).
    • Social Security: you can apply up to 4 months before you want payments to start (SSA).
    • Medicare: sign up during the 7 months around your 65th birthday to avoid a lifelong penalty. The window starts 3 months before your birthday month (Medicare: when coverage starts). See when to sign up for Medicare.

    If you're married, the application is also where you choose a survivor option. Under FERS, a full survivor annuity costs 10% of your annuity and pays your spouse 50% of your unreduced annuity for life (OPM: computation). Under PERS, the options depend on your plan. See the FERS survivor benefit and PERS survivor options.

A timeline to follow

WhenWhat to do
3 years outGet records and estimates; fix earnings and service errors; look into buying service
1 year outChoose a target date; price health insurance; plan how to take savings
6 months outGo to a retirement class; talk over survivor options with your spouse
3 months outApply for your pension; give notice; apply for Social Security if starting now
Age 64 and 9 monthsYour Medicare window opens

These are common rules of thumb, not official deadlines, except where the steps above say otherwise. Your HR office or retirement system may want more notice.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Leaving a few months before a key birthday. Retiring just before your full-benefit age can cost you for life. Check the exact date.
  • Rushing the survivor choice. If you're married, how your pension is paid after your death is usually a one-time choice. Talk it over with your spouse first.
  • Missing the Medicare window. A retiree plan or COBRA doesn't let you put off Medicare Part B without a penalty.
  • Assuming everything starts the same day. Your pension, savings account and Social Security can each start at different times. Plan the gaps.
  • Forgetting the first-check delay. A first pension payment can take weeks to months. Keep enough cash to cover the wait.
  • Ignoring work rules after retiring. Going back to work can affect Social Security before full retirement age, the FERS supplement, and PERS retirees working for a PERS employer. See the Social Security earnings test, the FERS supplement earnings test and working after retiring from PERS.

Common questions

How far ahead should I start planning to retire?

About three years out gives time to fix records, buy back service and compare dates. Even a few months ahead is enough to apply on time.

How do I get an official pension estimate?

PERS members can use Online Member Services or ask PERS in writing. Federal employees ask their agency's benefits or HR office.

Can I retire before I start Social Security?

Yes. Your pension and Social Security are separate. Many public employees retire first and start Social Security later.

What if I retire before 65?

You'll need health coverage until Medicare. Federal retirees can usually keep FEHB, and PERS retirees can buy a PERS retiree plan. Compare your choices.

Is there a quick way to see if I can afford to retire?

Our Can I retire? tool gives a quick answer with Social Security at 62, 67 and 70. The planner goes into more detail.

Do I need a financial adviser?

Not to get your official numbers: your retirement system and Social Security provide them for free. Some people also pay an adviser for help with savings and taxes.

General information, not advice. Rules change, and only your retirement system can give you an official estimate. Ask them before you choose a date.
