Tier One, Tier Two and OPSRP: what's the difference?

Rules checked October 18, 2026, against oregon.gov/pers

Short answer

Your group depends on when you started a PERS job. Tier One started before 1996, Tier Two from 1996 to August 28, 2003, and OPSRP on August 29, 2003 or later. Tier One and Tier Two have a richer formula (1.67% a year versus 1.5% for general service), earlier full-benefit ages, and an extra way to figure your pension called Money Match. Everyone has an IAP account.

How the Full Formula works

Your pension is your formula rate times your years of service times your final average salary. PERS figures final average salary as the higher of two numbers: the average of your three highest-paid years, or your total pay over your last 36 months divided by 36. For OPSRP, the three years must be in a row (PERS: Tier One/Tier Two benefit calculation, PERS: OPSRP statement FAQ). For example, a Tier Two teacher with 28 years and $81,000 final average salary gets 1.67% × 28 × $81,000 = $37,876 a year, about $3,156 a month, at full-benefit age.

Since 2020, pay above a yearly limit doesn't count. The limit for 2026 is $245,724 a year, and it rises each year with inflation (PERS: salary limit).

A few things can raise a Tier One or Tier Two final average salary that don't apply to OPSRP:

  • Vacation payout (Tier One only). A lump-sum payout of unused vacation at retirement counts in a Tier One final average salary, but not Tier Two or OPSRP.
  • Unused sick leave (Tier One and Tier Two). If your employer takes part in PERS's sick leave program, half of your net unused sick leave hours are added to your final average salary (PERS: employer guide on unused sick leave). See unused sick leave and your PERS pension.

Source for both: PERS: Benefit component comparisons.

What is Money Match?

Tier One and Tier Two members also have a member account built from contributions made before 2004. Money Match takes that balance, doubles it (your employer matches it), and turns it into a monthly payment using PERS's factor for your age: account × 2 × age factor. PERS pays whichever method gives you more (PERS).

  • Tier One member accounts are credited PERS's assumed earnings rate, currently 6.9% a year.
  • Tier Two accounts earn actual investment returns, which can go up or down. PERS says Money Match is rarely the higher amount for Tier Two members.

Formula Plus Annuity is a third method for Tier One members who made contributions before August 21, 1981. It pays 1.00% a year for general service (1.35% for police and fire) plus an annuity bought with the member account (PERS: Benefit component comparisons).

Some Tier One and Tier Two members also had part of their member account in the variable account, which PERS invested 100% in global stocks. Those dollars rise and fall with the market and feed into Money Match.

Example: the same career in each plan

Example

Alex is a made-up general service member who retires at full-benefit age with 25 years and a $75,000 final average salary. Here's the Full Formula pension under each plan:

  1. Tier One or Tier Two: 1.67% × 25 × $75,000 = $31,313 a year, about $2,609 a month. Full-benefit age is 58 (Tier One) or 60 (Tier Two).
  2. OPSRP: 1.5% × 25 × $75,000 = $28,125 a year, about $2,344 a month. Full-benefit age is 65, because Alex has fewer than 30 years.
  3. The gap: about $265 a month, plus Tier One and Tier Two reach the full amount years sooner.
  4. If Alex were Tier One with a large member account: PERS would also run Money Match (account × 2 × age factor) and pay whichever is higher.

In real life a Tier One member would usually have more years than an OPSRP member of the same age, so the gap is often wider.

VestingActive member in each of 5 calendar years5 calendar years with 600+ hours, or reaching normal retirement age
Cost-of-living raiseUp to 2%, with unused amounts carried overUp to 2%, no carryover
Payment options13 options, including lump-sum choicesSingle life, or full or half survivorship
Retiree health subsidiesRHIA and, for state retirees, RHIPANot available
  • Cost-of-living raises: PERS caps the yearly raise at 2%. Service earned up to October 1, 2013 gets up to 2%; later service gets 1.25% on the first $60,000 of yearly pension and 0.15% above that. When prices rise less than 2%, Tier One and Tier Two members bank the difference for later years. OPSRP members don't (PERS: cost-of-living adjustment).
  • Payment options: Tier One and Tier Two members choose one of 13 options, from a life-only pension to options that keep paying a beneficiary or pay out the member account as a lump sum (PERS: Tier One/Tier Two preretirement guide). OPSRP has a single life option and full or half survivorship, each with an "increase" version that goes up to the single life amount if your beneficiary dies first (PERS: OPSRP preretirement guide). See PERS survivor options.
  • Health subsidies: RHIA pays $60 a month toward a PERS Medicare plan for eligible Tier One and Tier Two retirees. RHIPA helps eligible state retirees not yet on Medicare, based on years of state service. OPSRP retirees can join PERS health plans but don't get these subsidies (PERS: understanding retiree health insurance). See PERS retiree health insurance.

The IAP: everyone's savings account

Since 2004, members' own 6% contributions go into the Individual Account Program (IAP) instead of the member account. Members of all three groups have one. If you earn more than a monthly threshold ($3,890 in 2026), part of that 6% is sent to an Employee Pension Stability Account that helps pay for your pension: 2.5% of pay for Tier One and Tier Two, and 0.75% for OPSRP (PERS: Employee Pension Stability Account). When you retire you can take the IAP as a lump sum, in installments over 5, 10, 15 or 20 years, or over your expected lifetime (PERS: What is the IAP?). See IAP payout options.

What to do next

  1. Confirm your plan. Your annual statement in Online Member Services shows whether you're Tier One, Tier Two or OPSRP.
  2. Check your member account. If you're Tier One or Tier Two, note the balance; it drives Money Match.
  3. Ask about sick leave. Find out whether your employer takes part in PERS's sick leave program.
  4. Get an estimate. Request a PERS estimate, which runs every method your plan allows.
  5. See your dates. Read when can I retire from PERS? or try the quick Can I retire? tool.

Common questions

How do I know if I'm Tier One, Tier Two or OPSRP?

It depends on when you first started a PERS job: before 1996 is Tier One, 1996 to August 28, 2003 is Tier Two, and August 29, 2003 or later is OPSRP. Your PERS annual statement shows it.

Is Tier One better than Tier Two?

Both use the same 1.67% formula. Tier One has an earlier full-benefit age (58 versus 60), a guaranteed rate on the member account, and for some, Formula Plus Annuity.

Do OPSRP members get Money Match?

No. OPSRP pensions use the formula only. OPSRP members have no member account; their contributions go to the IAP.

What is the Tier One assumed rate?

PERS credits Tier One regular member accounts with its assumed earnings rate, which PERS lists as 6.9%.

Does a lump-sum vacation payout raise my pension?

Only for Tier One. Tier Two and OPSRP final average salaries leave it out.

Is there a cap on the salary PERS counts?

Yes. For 2026 the limit is $245,724 a year, and it rises with inflation.
