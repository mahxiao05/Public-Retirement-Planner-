Oregon PERS

Oregon PERS explained: a complete guide for members near retirement

How the Oregon Public Employees Retirement System works, from which plan you're in to how your pension is figured and how to apply.

Rules checked October 18, 2026, against oregon.gov/pers · Reviewed by Max Ma

Short answer

PERS pays most retirees two things: a monthly pension for life, figured from your years of service and your pay, and an IAP savings account built from 6% of your salary. Your plan (Tier One, Tier Two or OPSRP) depends on when you started, and it sets your retirement age and formula.

Which plan you're in

PERS covers most Oregon state employees, teachers and school staff, and many city, county and special district workers. Your plan depends on when you first started a PERS job:

PlanStarted a PERS job
Tier OneBefore January 1, 1996
Tier TwoJanuary 1, 1996 to August 28, 2003
OPSRPAugust 29, 2003 or later

OPSRP stands for Oregon Public Service Retirement Plan. Police and firefighters in each plan have their own, earlier retirement ages and a higher formula. Compare the three plans side by side.

To get a pension you must be vested. Tier One and Tier Two members are vested after being an active member in each of five calendar years. OPSRP members are vested after five calendar years with at least 600 hours of work, or by working in a qualifying job at or after normal retirement age. Your IAP is vested from the start (PERS: Benefit component comparisons). A job generally has to need at least 600 hours a year to count toward PERS.

How your pension is figured

For most members, the pension is:

formula rate × years of service × final average salary

  • Formula rate: 1.67% a year for Tier One and Tier Two general service, 2.0% for police and fire. OPSRP is 1.5% for general service and 1.8% for police and fire.
  • Final average salary: the higher of your three best years of pay or your pay over your last 36 months. For OPSRP, the three best years must be in a row (PERS: OPSRP statement FAQ). Since 2020, pay above a yearly limit doesn't count; the limit for 2026 is $245,724 and it rises with inflation (PERS: salary limit).
  • Years of service: your retirement credit, in years and months.

Tier One and Tier Two members can get a boost to final average salary from unused sick leave, if their employer takes part in PERS's sick leave program: half of their net unused hours are added (PERS: unused sick leave guide). Tier One final average salary also includes a lump-sum vacation payout. OPSRP gets neither. See unused sick leave and your PERS pension.

Example

Dana is a made-up OPSRP general service member with 25 years of service. Dana's three best years in a row averaged $70,000, more than the last 36 months, so that's the final average salary.

  1. At 65 (full retirement age): 1.5% × 25 × $70,000 = $26,250 a year, about $2,188 a month.
  2. At 62, three years early: PERS's factor keeps 77.5%, so about $1,695 a month, and that's before counting the fewer years of service Dana would have by then.
  3. The IAP on top: Dana's IAP balance is paid separately, as a lump sum or installments.

A police officer in OPSRP with the same numbers would use 1.8% instead of 1.5%: $31,500 a year.

Money Match, for Tier One and Tier Two

Tier One and Tier Two members also have a member account built from contributions made before 2004. Money Match doubles that balance and turns it into a monthly payment. PERS figures your pension both ways and pays whichever is higher (PERS: Tier One/Tier Two benefit calculation).

The IAP savings account

Since 2004, every member's 6% of salary goes into an Individual Account Program (IAP) account instead of the pension. Many employers pay the 6% for their workers. The account rises and falls with PERS's investments.

Since July 1, 2020, if you earn more than a monthly threshold ($3,890 in 2026), part of that 6% goes into an Employee Pension Stability Account that helps pay for your pension: 2.5% of pay for Tier One and Tier Two, and 0.75% for OPSRP (PERS: member redirect). PERS says the redirect applies while the system is less than 90% funded, and it reported the system at 80% funded as of December 31, 2025.

When you retire, you can take your IAP as a lump sum, in installments over 5, 10, 15 or 20 years, or over your expected lifetime (PERS: What is the IAP?). A few rules to know:

  • Same day as your pension. Since January 1, 2011, you retire from your pension and your IAP at the same time.
  • How it's invested. IAP accounts are in target-date funds, and you can pick a different one each September. There's no guaranteed return, so the balance can fall until it's paid out.
  • Rollovers. PERS says only the one-time lump sum and the five-year installments can be rolled into an IRA or another plan. Installments must be at least $200 each (PERS: preretirement guide).
  • Taxes. Money you take before 59½ may face a 10% IRS penalty unless an exception applies. The IAP payment normally arrives within 120 days of retiring.

See IAP payout options for a deeper look.

When you can retire

PlanFull pension (general service)
Tier One58, or any age with 30 years
Tier Two60, or any age with 30 years
OPSRP65, or 58 with 30 years

General service members in every plan can retire as early as 55. Retiring before your full-pension age lowers the pension for life. One year early keeps about 91.8% of it, and three years early about 77.5% (PERS factors, January 1, 2026). See every group's ages, including police and fire.

The cut grows the earlier you go: five years early keeps about 65.7%, and ten years early, which only OPSRP members under 30 years can reach, keeps 44.0%. Because the cut is permanent, a few more months of work before a full-benefit birthday can be worth a lot.

Yearly cost-of-living raises

PERS raises retirees' pensions each year to keep up with prices in the West, by no more than 2%. The raise depends on when you earned your service:

  • Service earned up to October 1, 2013: up to 2% a year.
  • Service after that: 1.25% on the first $60,000 of yearly pension, and 0.15% on any amount above $60,000.
  • People who retired before October 1, 2013 get the full 2%.

The raise starts July 1 and shows up in the August 1 payment. For 2026, prices rose 2.67%, so the 2% cap applied. When prices rise less than 2%, Tier One and Tier Two retirees bank the difference to use in later low years; OPSRP retirees don't. OPSRP members who retired after August 1 of the year before get a partial first raise (PERS: cost-of-living adjustment).

Protecting your spouse

When you apply, you choose how your pension is paid. A pension for your life only pays the most each month but stops when you die. Options that keep paying a spouse or other beneficiary after you die pay less each month.

  • Tier One and Tier Two members choose one of 13 options. They include a life-only pension, options that pay your beneficiary the same amount or half of it for life, a 15-year certain option, and options that pay out your member account as a lump sum along with a smaller pension (PERS: Tier One/Tier Two preretirement guide).
  • OPSRP members choose single life, full survivorship or half survivorship. The survivorship options have an "increase" version that raises your payment if your beneficiary dies first (PERS: OPSRP preretirement guide).
  • Your spouse must agree. If you're married, your spouse must consent to your choice. Without consent, PERS pays a half survivor option to your spouse.
  • A short window to change. PERS says in most cases the option can't be changed more than 60 days after your first payment.

Compare the options with your spouse before you sign. Our planner shows what your spouse would have in each case, and PERS survivor options walks through them.

Health insurance and Social Security

  • Health insurance: PERS retirees can buy coverage through the PERS Health Insurance Program, including Kaiser Permanente and UnitedHealthcare plans for people not yet on Medicare (PERS: PHIP). Eligible Tier One and Tier Two retirees can get help with premiums: RHIA pays $60 a month toward a PHIP Medicare plan, and RHIPA helps eligible state retirees not yet on Medicare. OPSRP retirees don't get these subsidies. See PERS retiree health insurance and compare your choices before 65.
  • Medicare: once you're eligible, PHIP requires both Medicare Part A and Part B. See when to sign up for Medicare.
  • Social Security: most PERS jobs also pay Social Security tax, so most members get both. Your PERS pension no longer lowers your Social Security. Read why.

Working after you retire

You can work for a private employer with no PERS limits. For PERS employers, most limits on hours are lifted through December 31, 2034, though your employer may have its own rules. If you retire before your normal retirement age and want to work unlimited hours for a PERS employer, PERS says you first need a complete break of at least six full months (PERS: preretirement guide). See working after retiring from PERS.

How to apply

  1. Get a pension estimate through Online Member Services on oregon.gov/pers, or ask PERS for one in writing.
  2. Go to a PERS retirement education session and read the preretirement guide.
  3. Turn in your retirement application within the 90 days before your retirement date. Tier One and Tier Two members use Form 364.
  4. Tell your employer you're retiring, so it can let PERS know.

PERS says only the application and telling your employer are required (Tier One and Tier Two steps, OPSRP steps). Your retirement date is always the first of a month, and PERS says your first payment arrives within 92 days of it, back-dated to that date. You can call PERS at 888-320-7377.

What to do next

  1. Sign in to Online Member Services. Check your plan, retirement credit and IAP balance.
  2. Find your full-benefit date. Use when can I retire from PERS?
  3. Get two estimates. One at your earliest date and one at your full-benefit date.
  4. Decide how to take the IAP. Compare a lump sum, a rollover and installments.
  5. Talk over survivor options. Your spouse will need to sign.
  6. Check the big picture. Try the quick Can I retire? tool.

Common questions

How is my Oregon PERS pension calculated?

Formula rate × years of service × final average salary. Tier One and Tier Two general service use 1.67%, OPSRP 1.5%. Tier One and Tier Two also get a Money Match calculation, and PERS pays the higher amount.

What is the IAP?

The Individual Account Program is a savings account built from 6% of your pay since 2004. It's paid separately from your pension when you retire.

How much is the PERS cost-of-living raise?

Up to 2% a year. Service after October 1, 2013 gets 1.25% on the first $60,000 of yearly pension and 0.15% above that.

Can I retire from PERS and keep working?

Yes. Most PERS limits on working for PERS employers are lifted through 2034. Early retirees need a six-month break first to work unlimited hours.

Does my PERS pension reduce my Social Security?

No. The Social Security Fairness Act ended those reductions for benefits from January 2024 on.

How long does it take to get my first PERS payment?

PERS says within 92 days of your retirement date, back-dated to that date. The IAP is normally paid within 120 days.

Who do I call at PERS?

Member Services at 888-320-7377, 8:30 a.m. to 5 p.m. on weekdays.

General information, not advice. We aren't part of Oregon PERS. Ask PERS for an official estimate before you choose a date.
