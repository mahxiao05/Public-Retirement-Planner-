Social Security

Social Security explained for public employees

Who qualifies, how much you get, when to start, and how Social Security fits with a PERS or FERS pension.

Rules checked October 18, 2026, against ssa.gov, irs.gov, medicare.gov and cms.gov

Short answer

If your job paid Social Security tax for about 10 years, you'll get a monthly benefit for life on top of your pension. You can start it at any age from 62 to 70. Starting later pays more each month, and a public pension no longer reduces it. Benefits rise 3.6% in January 2027 to keep up with prices.

Do you qualify?

You need 40 credits, which is about 10 years of work that paid Social Security tax (SSA: credits). All FERS jobs and most Oregon PERS jobs pay it. Look for "Social Security" or "OASDI" on your pay stub.

You can earn up to 4 credits a year. In 2026 you get one credit for each $1,890 of covered earnings, and in 2027 for each $1,970 (SSA: 2027 fact sheet). So a part-time job in retirement can still add credits if you're a few short. If you don't have 40 credits of your own, you may still get a benefit as a spouse or widow(er).

How much you'll get

Your benefit comes from your 35 highest years of earnings that paid Social Security tax, adjusted for wage growth. Years with no earnings count as zero. The result is your primary insurance amount, the monthly amount you'd get at full retirement age. The easiest way to see your number is your statement at ssa.gov/myaccount, which shows your estimate at 62, at your full retirement age and at 70.

  • Only earnings up to a cap count. The cap is $184,500 in 2026 and $192,300 in 2027. You don't pay Social Security tax above it, and it doesn't raise your benefit.
  • Typical amounts. Social Security estimates the average retired worker will get $2,170 a month after the January 2027 raise. The most a worker retiring at full retirement age in 2027 can get is $4,188 a month.
  • Yearly raises. Benefits go up each January with prices. The raise for 2027 is 3.6%.

Source: SSA: 2027 fact sheet. Working one more year only raises your benefit if that year's earnings beat one of your current top 35 years, which is often true late in a career.

When to start

You can start at any age from 62 to 70. If you were born in 1960 or later, your full retirement age is 67. Starting at 62 pays 70% of your full amount for life, and waiting until 70 pays 124% (SSA: retirement age). Each year you wait past full retirement age adds 8%, called delayed retirement credits, until 70 (SSA: delayed retirement credits).

A pension can cover your bills while you wait, so many public employees retire first and start Social Security later. Weigh the choice in our full guide.

Example: a couple with pensions

Example

Pat and Lee are a made-up married couple, both born in 1962, so both have a full retirement age of 67. Pat, a retiring state employee, has a full-retirement-age amount of $2,400 a month. Lee's own amount is $1,000.

  1. Lee's choices: Lee can get Lee's own $1,000, or a spousal benefit of up to half of Pat's amount, $1,200, once Pat has started. Social Security pays the higher of the two, so if both start at 67, Lee would get $1,200 in total.
  2. If Pat waits to 70 instead: Pat's benefit becomes 124% of $2,400, or $2,976 a month. Lee's spousal amount stays at $1,200, because spousal benefits don't grow with Pat's delay, and Lee can't get it until Pat files.
  3. If Pat dies first: Lee, past full retirement age, can switch to a survivor benefit of 100% of what Pat was getting, $2,976, instead of $1,200.
  4. If Pat had started at 62: Pat would get 70% of $2,400, or $1,680, and Lee's survivor benefit would be based on that smaller check.

Raises and taxes are left out. The point: the higher earner's start age sets the survivor's income for life.

Working while you collect

If you start before full retirement age and keep working, Social Security holds back $1 for every $2 you earn over $25,560 in 2027. In the year you reach full retirement age, it holds back $1 for every $3 over $67,920, counting only months before your birthday month. From full retirement age on, there's no limit (SSA: earnings limits). The 2026 limits are $24,480 and $65,160. Only wages and self-employment income count; your pension doesn't. See the earnings test explained.

Your spouse and survivors

  • Spouse: a spouse can get up to half of your full-retirement-age amount while you're alive, if that's more than their own benefit. You must have filed for your own benefit first. Starting at 62 can cut the spousal benefit to as little as 32.5% of your amount (SSA: benefits for a spouse). See spousal benefits.
  • Widow or widower: after you die, your spouse can get 100% of your benefit at their full retirement age, or 71.5% to 99% if they start between 60 and full retirement age. They can take one benefit first and switch to the other later if that pays more. Remarrying after 60 doesn't end it (SSA: if you are the survivor). See survivor benefits.
  • One-time payment: a surviving spouse or child may get a lump sum of $255.

How it fits with your pension

  • No more cuts for public pensions. The Social Security Fairness Act ended the Windfall Elimination Provision and Government Pension Offset for benefits from January 2024 on (SSA). What that means for you.
  • FERS retirees before 62: the FERS supplement pays a stand-in for Social Security until 62, and it has its own earnings test. How the supplement works and the supplement earnings test.
  • Oregon PERS retirees: the pension and IAP are separate from Social Security. Many members retire from PERS first and start Social Security later. Oregon PERS explained.
  • Washington DRS and other systems: the same rules apply. Your Social Security depends only on the covered earnings on your record. Washington DRS explained.

A few mistakes come up again and again for public employees:

  • Assuming the pension replaces Social Security. If your job paid Social Security tax, you earned both, and you have to apply for Social Security separately.
  • Starting at 62 by default. If your pension covers your bills, waiting raises a payment that is adjusted for prices and that a surviving spouse may inherit.
  • Forgetting the years outside public work. Earnings from earlier private jobs, military service or self-employment all count toward your top 35 years if Social Security tax was paid.
  • Not planning for taxes. A pension plus Social Security often pushes part of the benefit into taxable income. Setting up withholding avoids a surprise in April.

Taxes on your benefit

Depending on your total income, part of your Social Security may be taxed, and a pension counts toward that income. Social Security says you may owe federal income tax on benefits if your combined income (half your benefits plus your other income) is over $25,000 filing alone or $32,000 filing jointly. You can ask Social Security to withhold 7%, 10%, 12% or 22% of each payment (SSA: income taxes and your benefit). At higher incomes, up to 85% of your benefit can be taxed. For 2025 through 2028, people 65 or older can take an extra $6,000 deduction, which phases out above $75,000 of income ($150,000 for joint filers) (IRS: enhanced deduction for seniors). See are Social Security benefits taxed?

Medicare at 65

Medicare starts at 65 no matter when you start Social Security. Sign up during the 7 months around your 65th birthday. Signing up late for Part B adds 10% to your premium for each full year you could have had it, for as long as you have it, unless you're covered by a current job (Medicare: avoiding penalties). The standard Part B premium is $202.90 a month in 2026, and higher incomes pay more through IRMAA (CMS: 2026 premiums). Once you get Social Security, the Part B premium is usually taken from your check. See when to sign up for Medicare.

How to apply

Apply online at ssa.gov, by phone at 1-800-772-1213, or at a local office (call first for an appointment). You can apply up to 4 months before you want payments to start (SSA: how to apply). If you're past full retirement age, back pay is limited to 6 months.

What to do next

  1. Open your statement. Sign in at ssa.gov/myaccount and check every year of earnings.
  2. Fix missing years now. Errors are easier to fix while you still have W-2s and the employer is easy to reach.
  3. Get your spouse's numbers too. Spousal and survivor benefits depend on both records.
  4. Decide on a start age. Compare 62, full retirement age and 70 with the quick Can I retire? tool.
  5. Mark Medicare. Put the 7-month window around your 65th birthday on your calendar.

Common questions

Can I get Social Security and a public pension at the same time?

Yes. Since the Social Security Fairness Act, a government pension doesn't reduce your Social Security or a spousal or survivor benefit.

How many years do I need to work for Social Security?

You need 40 credits, about 10 years of covered work. You can earn up to 4 credits a year.

What is the Social Security raise for 2027?

3.6%, starting with January 2027 benefits.

Is my Oregon PERS job covered by Social Security?

Most PERS jobs are. Check your pay stub for "Social Security" or "OASDI", or check your earnings record at ssa.gov.

Does my pension count against the earnings limit?

No. The earnings test counts wages and self-employment income, not pensions, IAP or TSP withdrawals.

Will my Social Security be taxed?

Possibly. If your combined income is over $25,000 (single) or $32,000 (joint), part of your benefit may be taxed.

Can my spouse get benefits on my record?

Yes, up to half of your full-retirement-age amount, once you've filed and they're at least 62.
